# THE AURELIAN LEAGUE
## Story Bible — Version 2.0
**Revised: November 5, 2026 | Status: Narrative redesign, approved premise**

> **Authority and implementation:** This document supersedes the November 4, 2026 version 1.2 story bible. It is the source of truth for new narrative planning. The currently published game, README, existing chapter scripts, and some older planning notes still represent the *previous* League-contestant story until those files are deliberately rewritten. Nothing described here should be mistaken for already playable content.
>
> **Labels:** **Established** means agreed story direction; **Open** means an undecided detail or a suggested approach. This document deliberately avoids inventing answers to open questions.

## 1. Project identity and premise

**Working title:** The Aurelian League  
**Format:** Choice-based Pokémon interactive fiction  
**Genre:** Character-driven adventure, mystery, fugitive story, moments of humor and warmth, increasingly serious criminal conspiracy  
**Setting:** Aurelia, a remote, Roman-inspired island region of the Pokémon world  
**Protagonist:** A customizable adult, junior League field warden, not a League contestant. They believe the League is their employer and do not know of its Team Rocket connection at the outset

**Established premise:** Team Rocket controls a small Pokémon League in Aurelia. Publicly it offers Pokémon training and competition to communities that have had little access to an established League. Privately, the League is a front for laundering money, stealing and transporting Pokémon, and supporting illicit research. People have been disappearing across the island. Rocket has learned to transform humans into Pokémon through research based on an unusual Ditto.

The protagonist believes they work directly for the League, performing low-level field-welfare duties such as recovering and relocating Pokémon. They do not know that Team Rocket controls the League; discovering the League's criminal activity aboard a ship comes well before learning who is behind it. An apparently ordinary capture-and-delivery assignment leads them aboard a cargo ship carrying imprisoned experimental subjects. They rescue one Pokémon, accidentally become a fugitive with direct knowledge of the trafficking operation, and eventually learn that their companion was once human.

The protagonist's initial goal is to escape and reach safety. The larger story leads toward helping an independent research institute expose the transformations, stop Rocket's operation, and save those victims who can still be restored.

The story is **not** organized around the protagonist earning eight Laurels, ranking against contestants, or entering the Triumvirate. Gyms, battles, and the League itself may feature in the narrative when appropriate, but no competition schedule governs the player's journey.

## 2. The experience we want to write

- The story should feel like a Pokémon adventure first, with opportunities to encounter, care for, travel with, and battle alongside Pokémon.
- The opening is comedic and grounded. The cargo-ship discovery changes the tone without eliminating warmth, humor, or everyday life from later chapters.
- The player's choices should shape the protagonist's responses, relationships, trust, discoveries, companion interactions, and significant later consequences.
- The protagonist may speak and think directly in the narration; they are not a silent blank slate. Choices must still accurately signal the attitude or action the player selects.
- Use natural, connected paragraphs and substantive conversations. Favor a few worthwhile choices over constant shallow menus. An approximate default is two or three choices and three to five paragraphs between Continue breaks where the pacing permits.
- No forced minigame grind, point economy, weekly timetable, or full turn-by-turn battle simulation is needed.
- Mystery should arise from observable clues and character behavior, not withholding facts the protagonist clearly knows.
- The emotional through-line is responsibility: the protagonist begins by helping deliver a victim into captivity, then tries to prevent others from suffering the same fate.

## 3. Aurelia: a remote island

**Established:** Aurelia is an island region with Roman and Mediterranean influences. It is sufficiently populated to sustain towns, rural communities, commerce, harbors, travel routes, and a small Pokémon League, yet sufficiently remote for an outside criminal organization to accumulate influence.

The look and culture may draw on stone harbors, colonnades, forums, aqueducts, old roads, orchards, hills, coastal villages, and Roman-inspired civic traditions. Modern Pokémon-world technology, transportation, and everyday infrastructure coexist with these older influences.

Ordinary Pokémon live throughout the region. **Not every Pokémon is a transformed human.** The experimental subjects are a particular set of victims, including those aboard the relevant shipment. This distinction is essential to keeping normal catching, partnerships, and exploration viable.

Many residents regard the new League as an opportunity for jobs and legitimate training. Some suspect wrongdoing; others are unaware or have reason to distrust accusations against an important local employer. Reported disappearances have increased, though the number, duration, authorities' response, and exact geographic pattern are **open**.

**Place-name continuity:** Lezano is an established name from the prior iteration and can be retained as a port if it suits the revised map. Marnetto, Miresto, Cadreno, Vettane, Calterro, Falcera, Belloro, and Castrenne were older proposals, **not locked locations or an approved new island map**. Revise or discard them as needed rather than forcing the old Laurel route onto the new story.

The independent research institute is expected to be beyond Rocket's local control, plausibly on the mainland. Its precise location, name, and jurisdiction remain **open**.

## 4. The public League and Rocket's operation

**Established:**

- The Aurelian League is small and remote, intended for trainers who otherwise have limited access to official competition. Its inaugural competitive season is already approximately halfway underway when the story begins. A season-based structure is credible for this region, though the exact length, scheduling, and gym format are open.
- The protagonist works directly for the League in a low-level Pokémon welfare/field warden role. They and other ordinary employees may genuinely believe their relocation assignments are legitimate; they are not knowingly Team Rocket personnel.
- Rocket uses the League's legitimacy, money flows, employees, facilities, and transport relationships for criminal purposes.
- Money laundering and the theft/trafficking of Pokémon are part of the operation.
- Rocket has also exploited the region and its infrastructure to conduct experiments transforming humans into Pokémon.
- Many staff members, local trainers, and potentially League officials are not aware of the full scheme. An affiliation with the League does not automatically make a character a villain.
- Rocket has begun treating transformed people as valuable Pokémon, particularly once it notices their unusual intellectual and physical potential.

**Open:** Whether Rocket founded the League or took control of a legitimate one; which officials know the truth; the precise accounting schemes, experimental sites, shipping destinations, buyers, and degree of local corruption. The plot needs specific answers eventually, but none should be treated as canon yet.

The League is a setting and institutional antagonist, not a progression system for the protagonist. Prior Laurel challenges, televised weekly recap episodes, fixed eliminations, ten-week season, and Triumvirate tournament plans are **superseded as required game structure**. Individual elements may be reimagined later only if they serve the new story.

## 5. The experimental Ditto and human transformation

**Established speculative-fiction rules:**

1. Team Rocket's breakthrough began with research involving an unusual Ditto whose Transform ability differs from that of ordinary Ditto. The unusual Ditto is a captive being, not the story's villain.
2. Rocket has found a way to transform a human's body into the body of a Pokémon, including the brain. The process is not a cosmetic disguise.
3. Memory and identity degrade **progressively**. Early on, fragments of human experience may be inaccessible, confused, or expressed as unexplained familiarity. As time passes, the transformed person loses explicit memories, language capacities, and their sense of having been human.
4. A person's broad disposition survives: protectiveness, curiosity, caution, pride, stubbornness, affection, and similar enduring traits can carry into their Pokémon behavior. Pokémon instincts and capabilities also influence how they behave.
5. Former humans are unusually intelligent and adaptive **for members of their new Pokémon species**, but not cognitively equivalent to intact adult humans. They cannot simply write an explanation or reliably tell someone who they used to be. Pokémon themselves are intelligent, and the comparison does not imply otherwise.
6. Transformed people have greater-than-normal potential for power and development. The advantage need not be immediately spectacular: unusual growth, tactical adaptability, and capabilities that stand out over time are preferable to instant overwhelming strength.
7. After sufficient time, the transformation **becomes irreversible**. There may be a window in which other victims can be restored. The exact period, biological test, and restoration method are **open**.
8. The protagonist's rescued starter ultimately **cannot be returned to human form**. The precise moment this becomes known is an outlining decision, not a player-timed failure condition.
9. A permanently transformed person retains a real present-day personality, capacity for attachment, and ability to live meaningfully as a Pokémon. That does not erase the tragedy of the human life taken from them.

**Open scientific/plot mechanisms:** What makes this Ditto unique, how Rocket extracts or reproduces the effect, whether the Ditto must be present for every transformation or reversal, why the greater power develops, how transformed subjects are identified, and what exactly locks the change into permanence. Establish these privately before writing detailed research revelations. Do not dress speculative plot mechanics up as real biological facts.

## 6. The protagonist

The player character is a **junior League field warden**, with comparatively little status or knowledge of the organization behind their employer. Their duties involve capturing or recovering Pokémon and delivering them for supposedly legitimate conservation and relocation work. The protagonist does **not** know at the beginning that Team Rocket controls the League. Their complicity is unknowing: they have helped carry out harmful assignments without recognizing their purpose. Their specific formal job title may be refined later.

The player can establish their name, pronouns, and attitude through the story. Their internal thoughts and spoken dialogue should be written as genuine characterization, with important emotional stances and moral responses left to player choice.

The protagonist is not a conventional new League entrant. Their development into a caring, competent trainer is part of the journey.

The revelation aboard the ship is the decisive break from the League as the protagonist understands it. The identity of Team Rocket as the force behind the scheme is a **later discovery**, not an opening exposition beat. Their first impulse is to release the imprisoned Pokémon; escaping with only one is the result of an interruption, not a decision that the others are expendable.

The people operating the trafficking scheme pursue them because they know too much, have taken an experimental subject, and could enable outsiders to prove the operation exists. The pursuers' Team Rocket connection is initially concealed from the protagonist. The protagonist's own involvement may complicate seeking help. We must establish convincing, noncontrived reasons for the particular danger and travel route rather than declaring all authorities corrupt by default.

## 7. Opening sequence: the first capture and the cargo ship

These beats are **established direction**, not final chapter boundaries or finished dialogue.

### A. Tall grass: a routine assignment with a comic tone

The protagonist and a **more senior League field warden** try to corner a small Pokémon in the grass. Both repeatedly miss it. The experienced grunt is sure this should be easy; the scene has room for physical comedy, impatience, and contrasting approaches to Pokémon handling.

The target **may be Chikorita**, which would tie the opening directly to one of the three rescue choices. The exact target species has not been finalized. One proposed explanation is that this Pokémon escaped an experimental facility and the wardens have been told to recapture it for a benign relocation.

The pair eventually capture it. The player can affect *how* they behave during the assignment without rewriting the fact that the delivery proceeds.

### B. The dock: the unattended delivery

**Both field wardens go to the ship.** The person responsible for checking in Pokémon is distracted while chasing a small escaped Pokémon. The senior warden joins the chase and tells the protagonist to leave their delivery up on the ship's deck.

After boarding, the protagonist notices a practical sign directing **“Pokémon to Holding Area”**, with an arrow toward the receiving area. Wanting to be helpful and not leave a Pokémon unattended, they follow the sign. The deeper cargo hold is discovered through that ordinary action, **not because the protagonist is already searching for a conspiracy**. The senior warden remains occupied helping the attendant.

### C. The hold: something is wrong

Belowdecks, the protagonist discovers cages containing numerous frightened, confined Pokémon. The relevant shipment consists of transformed human victims, although neither protagonist nor player understands this at the time. Details such as mismatched documents, an improper destination, or the condition of the captives can establish that the supposed relocation program is a lie.

The discovery should unfold from plausible observations. This is a strong location for **internal reflection choices**: disbelief, anger, fear, guilt, suspicion, or an impulse to look for a benign explanation. The selected internal reaction colors the protagonist's thoughts and later dialogue without preventing the decision to try freeing the Pokémon. The protagonist initially knows the League has deceived them; Team Rocket's involvement remains unknown.

### D. One cage opens

Three nearby choices are **Charmander, Chikorita, and Piplup**. The player chooses which to free *first*, intending to release more.

Before they can continue, someone approaches or enters the hold. An immediate threat forces the protagonist to escape with the one Pokémon already freed. The remaining prisoners and their fate matter to the wider story.

The escape and its immediate consequences still need scene-level design.

## 8. The three rescue partners

**Only these three are approved opening partner choices for the redesigned story:**

- **Charmander**
- **Chikorita**
- **Piplup**

Every choice is narratively valid and equally important. The starter selection does **not** involve the previous seven-Pokémon mutual-selection field, and no League official awards a starter.

**Established:** Each of these three is a human transformed into a Pokémon, with a distinct former identity and history. Each has better-than-typical intelligence and potential for its species, retains a recognizable disposition, has impaired or fading human memory, and ultimately cannot be returned to human form. Early behavior can be interpreted as ordinary individual Pokémon temperament, with a second meaning after the truth is revealed.

**Established foreshadowing:** In the early Starting Over sequence (working Scene 11), the **starter the player rescued** becomes unexpectedly affectionate with a person they meet. The protagonist wonders aloud whether they know each other. The person replies that they have never seen a Pokémon of that species before. Later it becomes clear that **the chosen starter is the person reported missing from that person's life**, transformed and unable to remember or communicate its former identity. This is not a different Pokémon belonging to that person.

**Open:** The three starters' former names, ages, human lives and relationships, why they were taken, the order of their transformations, and why this specific person or locale can trigger an emotional bond. Whichever starter the player chooses must have a credible former connection to the person they meet. The scene may use different supporting characters or relationship details depending on the chosen starter; do not force an implausible identical biography onto three distinct people. Keep the affection readable as Pokémon temperament on first encounter, not explicit proof of transformation. Define each starter's history before drafting these clues.

The rescued Pokémon becomes a major character, not a disposable battle tool or a plot coupon. Trust, consent, and the companion's own responses should matter. Once the truth is known, the story must address what their partnership means; their former humanity does not entitle the protagonist or anyone else to ownership of them.

The remaining two rescue candidates need not vanish from the plot. Their eventual fates are **open** and can supply later consequences or investigative leads.

## 9. Disappearances, the research institute, and pursuit

**Established:**

- People have been reported missing across Aurelia. Their disappearances connect to Rocket's transformation operation.
- An **independent Pokémon research/conservation group** has been investigating unusual disappearances, experiments, or shipments but has not been able to obtain a living transformed subject as proof.
- It learns that a junior League field warden escaped with a Pokémon taken from the operation and eventually makes contact.
- The group initially asks to take the rescued Pokémon to a research institute outside the League's control, claiming the examination may help bring down those running the League. Its representative **will not disclose the suspected transformation mechanism**, offering the limited explanation that an examination is needed before they can prove wrongdoing. The protagonist can challenge this evasiveness in substantial dialogue.
- **The Pokémon refuses to leave the protagonist.** Respecting this, the organization tells the protagonist where to go and offers to misdirect the people pursuing them while they travel. The institute becomes a destination only after the protagonist has struggled to start over and formed relationships through odd jobs.
- The institute's examination eventually exposes the human-to-Pokémon transformation. By the larger story's resolution, the starter's transformation is permanent.
- Rocket tries to recover the Pokémon and contain the witness. Pursuit can include investigations, uneasy encounters, personal pressure, and occasional direct confrontations. It should not become a compulsory chase scene every chapter.
- The larger threat is Rocket's ability to expand, commercialize, or ship experimental subjects beyond the island. Stopping that operation and rescuing as many people as possible gives the story its ultimate objective.

**Open:** The group's name, credibility, specific contact method, who personally approaches the protagonist, route to the institute, sequence of scientific discoveries, the nature of possible reversals, and the concrete threat that requires a return to Aurelia.

The institute may be fundamentally benevolent while having its own priorities. Its members need not agree on what risks are justified, and their treatment of the rescued companion should be subject to ethical scrutiny.

## 10. Recurring cast

Aim for approximately **five or six** substantial recurring human characters, plus important Pokémon personalities.

**Roles to develop, not assigned names:**

1. **The senior League field warden:** The protagonist's supervisor or more experienced colleague in the opening, held responsible for a junior employee disappearing during an assignment they oversaw. **They actively seek the protagonist out because they are worried about losing their job** or being blamed for the incident. Their initial questions and urgency come from self-preservation rather than an assignment to hunt down a fugitive. The protagonist cannot immediately tell if they can be trusted, but they **are trustworthy at heart**, though their help is limited, imperfect, and sometimes comically ineffective. They do not initially know about Rocket's secret control of the League. Their exact employment and knowledge once that connection is exposed remain open.
2. **The research contact:** A representative of the independent group with a personal or professional reason to pursue the disappearances.
3. **An institute researcher:** A source of credible findings, difficult decisions, and revelations about the transformed Pokémon.
4. **A farmer or other local resident:** The protagonist hides in a barn after fleeing the harbor; the farmer discovers them and opens a conversation about hardship. The player decides how candid to be. An offer of honest work and food/lodging starts the protagonist's new life, and the farmer may remain a significant ally.
5. **A significant Rocket antagonist:** Someone with actual power over the operation who knows enough to regard the protagonist as dangerous.
6. **An optional close companion/rival:** Someone whose relationship with the protagonist develops through travel or local encounters.

These are **story functions**, not a demand to introduce six separate characters. Combine functions when it improves the cast. Names, exact loyalties, romances, and individual arcs remain open. Characters should have lives and motivations beyond the protagonist's immediate needs.

## 11. Long-form narrative shape

The high-level progression is established, but exact acts, chapter count, locations, and scenes are for a separate interactive-fiction outline.

**Opening / rupture:** The comedic capture, delivery, disturbing discovery, choice of partner, and forced escape.

**Flight and new attachments:** The protagonist hides in a barn and is found by a farmer. Dialogue about hardship lets the player decide how much of their past to reveal. A few **odd jobs** provide meals, beds, basic supplies, new Pokémon encounters, and friendships without a numerical money or wage system. The protagonist learns to travel with their partner while unaware of the League's Rocket connection, and gradually notices pursuit. **In the working Scene 11, the rescued starter** behaves with unexpected affection toward a local person. When the protagonist asks whether the two know each other, the person says they have never seen a Pokémon of that species before. A disappearance connected to this person's life is mentioned; the player does not yet know that the **starter is that missing human**. The senior warden also tracks the protagonist down, worried that the junior's disappearance on their assignment will cost them their job. Their encounter should create uncertainty without portraying the warden as secretly treacherous.

**A destination and a mystery:** The research group claims a lab examination could bring down those running the League, but initially declines to reveal its deeper suspicions. The rescued Pokémon refuses to leave the protagonist; the representative gives them a route to the institute and promises to throw their pursuers off the trail. Clues about island disappearances and the companion accumulate.

**The revelation:** Researchers establish that the Pokémon was formerly human and uncover the transformation process and the limits of reversing it.

**Return and reckoning:** The protagonist has an opportunity to help stop the program and save victims who are not yet beyond recovery.

**Resolution:** The fate of Rocket's operation, other victims, the protagonist's relationships, and the companion's future respond to meaningful earlier choices. The companion remains permanently transformed, but that need not prevent a hopeful, freely chosen future.

Do not turn these headings into rigid mandatory chapters. Allow room for quiet travel, community life, catching ordinary Pokémon, small mysteries, and moments of humor between dramatic events.

## 12. Interactive fiction and gameplay boundaries

- **Narrative spine with responsive branches.** Major turning points can reconverge while previously chosen attitudes, allies, companion species, discoveries, promises, and mistakes change later scenes and endings.
- **Meaningful choices.** Offer social and ethical agency and selective route/encounter variation. Avoid multiple options with indistinguishable consequences, arbitrary gotchas, or hidden requirements that make a long playthrough unwinnable.
- **Relationships.** A hidden 1–20 relationship scale with a neutral starting point near 10 is an approved *design direction*, but particular narrative events and broken promises may also require explicit flags. Numbers should not be displayed to the player.
- **Pokémon battles.** Battles can occur when dramatically appropriate and should be written as narrative encounters, not as a complete HP/accuracy/damage simulation. Earlier discussion favored selecting one Pokémon for an individual battle and simple level progression per use; specifics must be confirmed when combat is actually implemented.
- **Catching and equipment.** Conventional Poké Balls are preferred over the previous League-bracelet-only system. Capture mechanics, available types of Balls, and inventory effects are not yet fully specified.
- **No currency ledger.** The protagonist is practically penniless after escaping, but the game does **not** track credits or require repeated payments. Odd jobs exchange work for shelter, meals, transport assistance, basic Poké Balls, and occasional notable items. Use inventory only for items whose possession meaningfully affects scenes.
- **Character interface.** Visible Stats and Inventory are probably sufficient alongside story and Pokémon/party information. Journal and Skills can remain underlying engine capabilities or future optional modules without appearing in this game's main UI.
- **Saves.** During early development, a published game-version mismatch should require a new game rather than migrations or backward compatibility. The versioning mechanism belongs to a later code task.
- **Future visuals.** Pokémon sprites are deferred; Emerald-era pixel art is a visual reference preference, not a current production requirement. Existing Pokémon imagery has intellectual-property implications that must be considered before public distribution.
- **Future modular engine.** A later Codex project may separate generic IF systems from Pokémon-specific features and story content. Do not let that speculative architecture dictate the current writing.

These statements are *narrative requirements or future technical intentions*. They do not assert that the present engine implements new mechanics.

## 13. Superseded v1.2 concepts

The following are **no longer canon for the redesigned story**:

- The player as a first-time League contestant on a ferry to Lezano.
- A field of seven mutually selecting starter Pokémon after League orientation.
- An eight-Laurel progression with non-battle trials, weekly schedules or point totals.
- Qualification for the Triumvirate as the protagonist's main objective.
- The old television-production conspiracy as the central mystery.
- Prior compulsory locations, contestant relationships, and chapter routes.
- The mandatory silver League bracelet as a replacement for Poké Balls.

The old playable chapters and their associated characters remain in Git history and current application content only until rewritten. Do not silently import their events into the new story.

## 14. Open decisions to settle before detailed prose

1. **Former identities:** Who were Charmander, Chikorita, and Piplup, and what fragments of their past survive?
2. **Transformation rules:** What makes the Ditto unique? Can Rocket perform or reverse transformations without it? How long until the process is irreversible, and how can the institute tell?
3. **Opening logistics:** Which Pokémon is chased in the grass, how it was assigned, who interrupts the hold rescue, and how the protagonist leaves the harbor. The senior warden accompanies the protagonist to the ship and is distracted helping the attendant chase an escaped Pokémon.
4. **Geography:** Aurelia's island map, the exact Rocket-controlled locations, the institute's location, and the necessary travel route.
5. **Character functions:** Senior League warden, pursuer, research contact, lead researcher, farmer or other civilian ally, and major Rocket antagonist.
6. **Proof and stakes:** What the institute can establish from one living subject; what additional evidence is required; what imminent shipment or deployment makes intervention urgent.
7. **Ethical consequences:** How the protagonist and institute handle the companion's autonomy, and what the former person's family learns.
8. **Outcome variants:** Which independent objectives can succeed or fail without undercutting the central story; what happens to the captive Ditto and the remaining victims.
9. **Mechanics:** Exact battle/capture representation and which hidden story variables are genuinely necessary. The new story will not use numerical credits.
10. **The missing-person clue:** For each selected starter, who is the person who attracts its unexpected affection in Scene 11, what was their relationship to the starter's former human identity, and how can the connection be revealed convincingly at the institute? The starter itself is the missing person, not a separate Pokémon in the household. Decide whether to branch the local NPC or tie each former identity to a shared community without straining coincidence.

## 15. Working method

Keep this bible about **facts and rules of the story world**. Maintain a separate **plot outline** for the sequence of major events and an optional **scene outline** for each chapter. Do not duplicate fixed story facts across all three documents.

Outline the critical path from beginning to end first. Then add meaningful alternative approaches, relationship consequences, companion-specific details, and optional scenes. Draft prose only after the main structural logic and mystery reveal sequence work.
